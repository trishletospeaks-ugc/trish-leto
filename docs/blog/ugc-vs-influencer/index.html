UGC vs Influencer Marketing: What You Are Really Paying For

By Trish Leto · October 1, 2026 · 8 min read

The short answer on UGC vs influencer

Here is the whole UGC vs influencer question in one line: a UGC creator makes content for your channels, and an influencer posts content on theirs. That split is not a law or a platform rule. It is how the industry uses the words, and Hootsuite (opens in a new tab), a social media software company, puts it exactly that way on its blog.

Think of it in GenX terms. UGC is your mixtape. You pick the songs, you own the cassette, and you play it in any car you want. An influencer is a shout-out on somebody else's radio show. Huge reach, real fans, but it is their station and their playlist.

So everything comes down to one question: whose audience do you want? If you want your own buyers to see a real person using your product, in your ads and on your product pages, you are paying for content. If you want someone else's followers to hear about you from a voice they already trust, you are paying for reach. Neither one is wrong. They answer different questions, and mixing them up is how a budget goes sideways.

I'm Trish, a GenX UGC creator, model and Navy veteran in Tampa. I make the content side of this, so weigh my take with that in mind. I will still tell you when an influencer is the better call, because honesty first is not negotiable for me.

What you are paying for: content vs reach

When you hire a UGC creator, the deliverable is the video file. You get the footage, you post it or run it where you choose, and the people who see it are yours: your followers, your site visitors and whoever your ads reach. The video works on your schedule, not on someone else's posting calendar.

When you hire an influencer, the deliverable is a post on their account, seen by their audience. You are renting attention that someone else built. That attention is the product, and sometimes it is exactly what you need.

A brand-made video sits in a third lane. Your team or your agency shoots it, it runs on your channels, and it sounds like the brand talking, because it is. Here is how the three stack up side by side.

The disclosure row comes from the FTC, which I cover further down.

When UGC wins

UGC is usually the stronger fit when one of these sounds like you:

  • You run ads. Testing needs options. A batch of videos gives you several hooks and angles to try instead of betting the whole month on one.
  • You need content for pages you control. A real person holding, opening and using your product can go on product pages, in emails and on your own social accounts.
  • You want to keep the asset. With the right usage terms in writing, the video keeps working for you after the post date, wherever you are allowed to run it.
  • Your buyers are grown-ups. This is the one I care about most.

Here is why the grown-up point matters. In Pew Research Center's 2025 survey of US adults, among people ages 50 to 64, 85% use YouTube, 74% use Facebook, 40% use Instagram and 30% use TikTok, per its Social Media Fact Sheet (opens in a new tab).

Read that chart again if you sell to people in their fifties and early sixties. TikTok is my home turf, and even I will tell you that in this age group Pew puts TikTok at 30% and YouTube at 85%. If an influencer's big following lives somewhere your buyers only visit now and then, you are renting the wrong room.

With UGC, you run the video where your buyers already are, on YouTube, Facebook, Instagram or TikTok, instead of renting one creator's audience on one platform. The same batch can feed all of them, as long as your usage terms cover it.

When an influencer wins

I would be lying if I said UGC is always the answer, and lying is not my brand. An influencer is the better buy when:

  • You need their audience, not just a video. If nobody knows your brand yet, someone else's followers can be the fastest way in the door.
  • You have a launch moment. A coordinated push across several creators' feeds can make a launch feel like an event instead of a whisper.
  • You want social proof on their channel. A creator saying your name to people who already follow them, with comments and shares under it, is something a file in your Google Drive cannot do by itself.

The hybrid: creator posts run as ads

There is also a middle path, and it is worth knowing about. TikTok Spark Ads (opens in a new tab) let advertisers run organic TikTok posts as ads, including posts made by other creators with their authorization. Meta's version is called Partnership Ads. In May 2025, Meta (opens in a new tab) said advertisers can now feature just one partner in the ad's header on Facebook and Instagram, where before both the advertiser and the partner were listed.

That means you can take a creator's post and put ad money behind it, so it reaches people beyond their followers. The creator has to sign off, and the terms should be in writing. I break down the TikTok side in how TikTok Spark Ads work.

Rights and disclosure work differently

Two things are worth getting right before anyone hits record: who owns the video, and who has to say it is an ad.

Who owns the video

Under US copyright law, the person who actually created a work is ordinarily its author, and works made for hire are the exception, per Circular 30 from the U.S. Copyright Office. In plain English: unless your written deal says otherwise, the creator usually starts out holding the rights to the video they filmed.

So a UGC deal should say, in writing, where you can run the video, for how long, and whether paid ads are included. The same goes for an influencer's post. If you want to reuse it in your own ads later, that takes their permission too. This is general information, not legal advice, so loop in your own attorney on the contract. I walk through the checklist in UGC usage rights: what to get in writing.

Who has to disclose

The FTC is clear that free stuff counts. Its guide Disclosures 101 for Social Media Influencers (opens in a new tab) tells creators that if a brand gives them free or discounted products or other perks and they mention one of its products, they need to disclose it. That covers paid posts and gifted posts alike. For video, the same guide says the disclosure belongs in the video itself, not only in the description.

And this is not only the creator's problem. Under the FTC's Endorsement Guides, advertisers are subject to liability (opens in a new tab) for misleading or unsubstantiated statements made through endorsements, and for failing to disclose unexpected material connections with their endorsers. So whether you hire a UGC creator or an influencer, the claims in the video have to be true, and the brand connection has to be clear whenever a creator posts it.

How to choose: a 5-question checklist

Grab a pen. Five questions, honest answers.

  1. What is the goal? Getting in front of a brand-new crowd points to an influencer. Creative for your ads, pages and emails points to UGC.
  2. Which channel? If the video lives on your accounts and in your ads, that is UGC. If it needs to live on someone else's feed, that is an influencer.
  3. What is the budget shape? A steady supply of fresh videos every month fits a UGC retainer. One big push around a date fits a sponsored post or a creator campaign.
  4. What rights do you need? Organic only or paid ads too, which platforms, and for how long. Write it down before anyone films.
  5. What is the timeline? If you need videos to test this month and next month, plan a batch. If you need a splash on launch day, book creator feeds early.

If you answered "both" more than once, you are not indecisive. You might need both, and the hybrid above, a creator's post run as a Spark Ad or a Partnership Ad, is one way to bridge them.

Questions people ask

Is a UGC creator the same as an influencer?

Not quite. In common industry usage, a UGC creator makes content for your brand's channels and ads, while an influencer posts content on their own channels to their own audience. Some people do both jobs, so ask which one you are buying.

Do UGC creators post the videos on their own accounts?

Not by default. A standard UGC job delivers video files that you post or run as ads. Some creators will also post on their own feed or authorize a creator post for a TikTok Spark Ad, but that is a separate ask and belongs in your written agreement. For me, it is an ask.

Do UGC videos need an #ad disclosure?

When a creator posts about a brand that gave them pay, free product or perks, the FTC says to disclose it, and in a video the disclosure belongs in the video, not only in the description. When your brand runs the video as its own ad, the claims in it still have to be true. This is general information, not legal advice.
