Florida NIL Rules for High School Athletes: A Parent's Guide

By Trish Leto · October 1, 2026 · 9 min read

If your kid plays high school sports in Florida and somebody just said the words "NIL deal," you probably have three questions. Is this allowed? What can go wrong? And who exactly is this person sliding into the DMs? Fair questions, all three, and the Florida NIL rules answer most of them. I'm Trish, a GenX UGC creator and model in Tampa. Making short videos for brands is my job, so the content side of NIL is my lane. The legal side is not, so this guide sticks to what the rule books actually say and links to every one of them so you can read them yourself.

Quick ground rules for this page: this is general information, not legal or tax advice. I am not an athlete agent or an attorney, and I don't find, negotiate or sign deals for anyone. Eligibility questions go to your school's athletic director. Legal questions go to a Florida attorney.

Yes, Florida high school athletes can earn from NIL

The Florida High School Athletic Association (FHSAA) allows it. Under FHSAA Bylaw 9.9.4 (opens in a new tab), a student-athlete may profit from their name, image and likeness as long as they follow Bylaw 9.9. The bylaw lists commercial endorsements, promotional activities, social media presence, and product or service ads as permitted activities.

One honest caveat. The bylaw text I'm working from is the 2024-25 FHSAA Handbook, revised February 20, 2025. The FHSAA's June 2026 board recap (opens in a new tab) says the board approved bylaw amendments from two state bills, SB178 and SB538, for the 2026-27 school year, and it lists no NIL changes. Still, handbooks get updated. Ask your athletic director for the current version before anybody signs anything.

What is off limits for high school NIL

This is where well-meaning families get into trouble, so let's slow down.

School logos, uniforms and championships

Bylaw 9.9.4.3 bans using the uniforms, logos, mascots, insignia or identifying marks of the school, the FHSAA or the NFHS in any NIL activity. Making NIL money with the school's uniform, equipment, logo or name is also out unless the school, district or association gives prior written consent. The same section adds two more rules: no promoting third-party brands during school or FHSAA activities, and no using school accolades or championships in paid NIL work.

Translation for content: film in a plain shirt, somewhere that isn't shouting the school's name. The team hoodie stays in the closet for the brand video.

Nine product categories

Bylaw 9.9.4.4 lists nine categories a high school athlete cannot touch:

  1. Adult entertainment products and services
  2. Alcohol, tobacco, vaping and nicotine products
  3. Cannabis products
  4. Controlled substances
  5. Prescription pharmaceuticals
  6. Gambling, including sports betting, the lottery, and betting tied to video games, online games and mobile devices
  7. Weapons, firearms and ammunition
  8. Political or social activism
  9. NIL collectives

For what it's worth, I turn down alcohol, tobacco and gambling work in my own UGC business, so parts of this list feel very familiar.

Recruiting, transfers and school connections

Bylaw 9.9.4.6 says NIL can't be used to pressure, urge or entice a student to attend a school for athletics, and a deal can't be a disguise for recruiting. If an "NIL offer" seems to come with a school attached, that's your cue to call the athletic director.

Transfers have their own rule. Under Bylaw 9.9.4.7, an athlete who transfers after starting a sport can't secure an NIL agreement that season unless they meet an exception in Bylaw 9.3.2.2. And Bylaw 9.9.4.5 limits who connected to a school can be involved in NIL activity, so if an offer comes from someone tied to the athletic program, like a coach, a booster or a team donor, ask the athletic director before anything else.

The paperwork: contract, affidavit and penalties

A written agreement that ends at graduation

Under Bylaw 9.9.4.1, an NIL agreement is a fully executed, written contract. It is limited to the athlete's high school eligibility and cannot run past their high school graduation date. By signing, the athlete and parents also release the school, the district and the FHSAA from liability tied to the deal, and agree to hold them harmless. Read that part twice.

No handshake deals. No "we'll figure it out later" in the DMs. If it isn't written and signed, it isn't an NIL agreement under the bylaw.

The Affidavit of Compliance, within 5 business days

Bylaw 9.9.5 requires any student who does NIL activity to sign an Affidavit of Compliance. The student and parent must complete it, collect every signature and submit it to the school within five business days of signing the NIL agreement. Five business days goes by faster than a Friday night game when there's practice and homework in the mix. Put it on the calendar the same day you sign.

Penalties escalate

Bylaw 9.9.6.3 sets escalating sanctions when a student-athlete breaks Bylaw 9.9:

  • First offense: a formal warning. If it applies, the athlete must also end or change the deal, take down the posts and return what they received.
  • Second offense: ineligible to represent any member school for one year from the date the violation is discovered.
  • Third offense: ineligible for any interscholastic contest, in any sport, for the rest of high school.

The same bylaw says an athlete who falsifies information or receives an impermissible benefit is ruled ineligible under other bylaws. Honesty on that affidavit is not optional.

Who can represent your athlete under Florida NIL law

This is the part I care about most, because it's where a lot of shiny pitches live.

Florida's athlete agent definition is broad. Under section 468.452, Florida Statutes (opens in a new tab), an "athlete agent" includes anyone who recruits or solicits a student athlete into an agent contract, or who, for financial gain, tries to get promotional fees for a student athlete with any promoter who markets the athlete's athletic ability or reputation. That definition does not include a spouse, parent, sibling, grandparent or guardian. So, parents: helping your own kid is not agent activity under that definition.

Now the license. Section 468.453(8) (opens in a new tab) says a person must hold a valid athlete agent license to represent an intercollegiate athlete on NIL contracts. That rule is written for college athletes. But the statute's definition of "student athlete" includes a Florida resident who has told a college in writing that they intend to play there, so a committed senior may be covered. Ask a Florida attorney before anyone acts as your athlete's rep.

The stakes are real:

  • Doing business as an unlicensed athlete agent, or knowingly helping someone do it, is a third-degree felony (opens in a new tab).
  • An agent contract between a student athlete and an unlicensed person is void and unenforceable (opens in a new tab).
  • The Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation (opens in a new tab) licenses and regulates athlete agents, so that's where you check a license.

On the high school side, Bylaw 9.9.2 lists hiring a registered agent to manage an athlete's career among the things that may affect amateur status, with an exception for advising on NIL matters. If someone offers to "manage" your athlete, ask the athletic director how that bylaw applies before you sign a thing.

Where does that leave me? I'm not an agent. I don't pitch, negotiate, sign or take a cut of anyone's deal. I teach the content side, and I'll explain exactly what that means below.

What about new NIL laws?

You'll see headlines. Here's where two big ones stand as of October 1, 2026:

  • Florida HB 981 (2025), "Athlete Representation and Compensation," died in the House Commerce Committee (opens in a new tab) on June 16, 2025. Not law.
  • The federal Protect College Sports Act (S.4668) passed the U.S. Senate 77-22 (opens in a new tab) on September 28, 2026. It still has to get through the House. Not law.

Bills change. Bylaws change. Check the date on anything you read about NIL, including this page.

Where content fits in a high school NIL deal

Here's the fun part. A lot of NIL money comes down to content. In Opendorse's July 2025 report (opens in a new tab), 80% of the commercial NIL dollars athletes received came from services such as posts, versus 10% from products like jerseys and video games. Opendorse is a commercial NIL platform, so treat this as one company's data, not the whole market.

So if your athlete does NIL at all, the skill that matters is making a good short video that follows the rules. That takes two habits from day one.

Disclose every gifted or paid post

Free stuff counts. The FTC's Endorsement Guides say a material connection can include free or discounted products (opens in a new tab), even when the brand didn't require a post in return. The FTC's influencer guide also says to skip vague tags like "sp," "spon" or "collab" (opens in a new tab) and stand-alone words like "thanks" or "ambassador." Say it plainly, in the post itself.

And a note from my own lane: when I show a product in my videos, that doesn't mean the brand hired me. Same goes for your athlete. A product showing up in a post is not a sponsorship, so nobody should claim one that doesn't exist, and nobody should hide one that does.

Treat NIL money as income

The IRS says that if you have gig or sharing economy income (opens in a new tab), you must include all of it, whether or not a Form 1099-K shows up. I'm not a tax pro, so talk with one about how NIL cash and free product should be handled.

A parent's Florida NIL checklist

Before signing

  • Get the current FHSAA Handbook from your athletic director and read Bylaw 9.9 together.
  • Check the brand against the nine prohibited categories.
  • Make sure the deal is in writing and ends by graduation.
  • If the offer comes from anyone tied to the school's athletic program, ask the athletic director first.
  • If someone wants to represent your athlete, check their license with DBPR and talk to a Florida attorney.

While posting

  • No school logos, uniforms, mascots, accolades or championships.
  • No promoting brands during school or FHSAA activities.
  • Disclose every paid or gifted post, clearly, inside the post.

After signing

  • Submit the Affidavit of Compliance within five business days.
  • Keep copies of the contract, the affidavit and every post.
  • Track income for tax time.

Questions people ask

Can Florida high school athletes get NIL deals?

Yes. FHSAA Bylaw 9.9.4 lets a student-athlete profit from their name, image and likeness as long as they follow Bylaw 9.9. That includes a written agreement that ends by graduation, no prohibited categories, and an Affidavit of Compliance filed with the school within five business days of signing. Confirm the current handbook with your athletic director.

Can my child wear the school jersey or logo in an NIL post?

Not without permission. Bylaw 9.9.4.3 bars using school, FHSAA or NFHS uniforms, logos, mascots or marks in NIL activity unless the school, district or association gives prior written consent. The simplest move is to film in plain clothes, away from school branding.

Does a high school NIL deal have to be in writing?

Yes. Under Bylaw 9.9.4.1, an NIL agreement is a fully executed, written contract, and it cannot extend beyond the athlete's high school graduation date.

Can a parent help with NIL?

Yes. Florida's athlete agent definition in section 468.452 does not include a parent, spouse, sibling, grandparent or guardian, and the FHSAA affidavit is completed by the student and parent together. Violations still count against the athlete under Bylaw 9.9.6.3, so help them follow every rule. For legal questions, talk to a Florida attorney.
