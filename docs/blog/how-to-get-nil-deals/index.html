How to Get NIL Deals: Start With Content, Not a Pitch

By Trish Leto · October 1, 2026 · 8 min read

Here's my take on NIL, from a GenX UGC creator who makes short product videos: an NIL deal is a content job with paperwork attached. A brand pays you because your name, face and posts help it sell something. So if you want to know how to get NIL deals, start with the content and the rules, not with a pitch.

Quick ground rules from me before we start. I'm a content coach, not an agent, attorney or CPA. Nothing here is legal or tax advice, and nobody can promise you a deal. Your school's compliance office is your first call on anything about eligibility. Now let's get you ready.

Start with the rules (yes, before the fun part)

I know. Nobody ever went viral reading a compliance page. But college sports rules changed fast, and a deal that breaks them can cost you more than it pays.

On June 6, 2025, a federal judge approved the House v. NCAA settlement, according to the College Sports Commission (opens in a new tab), the group that runs the NIL Go portal. That settlement lets participating schools share revenue directly with athletes, and it came with a reporting system for deals with outside companies.

Here's the part most athletes need. Every NCAA Division I athlete must report third-party NIL deals worth $600 or more in total to NIL Go within five business days of signing or agreeing on payment terms, per the CSC's NIL page (opens in a new tab). That $600 includes deals that could reach it through royalties, contingencies or bonuses. And it applies whether or not your school opted in to revenue sharing (opens in a new tab).

Two scope notes. These CSC rules cover Division I only. Division II, Division III, NAIA and junior college athletes have their own rules, and Florida high school athletes follow the FHSAA. So your first move, before you pitch anybody, is a conversation with your school's compliance office, or your athletic director in high school.

What makes a deal clear NIL Go

NIL Go checks every reported deal against two tests.

A valid business purpose

The deal has to use your name, image and likeness to promote goods or services sold to the general public for profit. The CSC looks for proof of that promotion, industry-standard practice and direct activation of your NIL. In plain English: you actually show up and do the thing. That's why I love content deals. A post or a video is activation you can point to.

The CSC also warns about "warehousing." If a school-linked collective or booster pays for your rights without explaining how it will use them, that may not satisfy the rules.

A reasonable range of compensation

The second test asks whether the pay fits what someone in a similar spot would earn. The CSC weighs the deal's deliverables, your athletic performance, your social reach, your local market and your school's market reach.

Here's the July 2026 update a lot of older articles miss. Deals from $600 to $15,000 now skip range-of-compensation review until you reach $50,000 in associated deals in an academic year. If an article gives you different numbers, check its date. Rules move, so read the CSC page yourself before you sign anything.

If a deal is not cleared

You can revise and resubmit, cancel the deal and refund, or appeal to neutral arbitration. If you go ahead with the deal as submitted, the CSC says you may face enforcement, which could include loss of eligibility. No sponsorship is worth your eligibility. Not one.

NIL deals for athletes: the market right now

The rules haven't slowed the money down. Athletes have cleared 46,478 NIL deals worth $582.49 million since NIL Go opened in June 2025, according to the CSC's September 2026 data release (opens in a new tab). July and August 2026 alone added 12,283 deals worth $227.25 million, the biggest two-month stretch so far.

Another 2,296 deals worth $156.93 million were not cleared. That's exactly why the rules section came first.

Keep the scale in perspective. NIL Go only counts Division I deals of $600 or more, so this is one slice of the NIL world, not all of it.

Content is the product

Here's the stat that matters most for content. Opendorse, a commercial NIL platform reporting on its own marketplace, found that athletes receive just 10% of commercial NIL dollars tied to products (opens in a new tab) such as jerseys and video games, but 80% of the dollars tied to services such as posts. Read that again. When a brand pays for content, most of that money lands with the athlete.

That's my lane. Talking heads, yap-style videos, lifestyle clips, product demos and b-roll are the same skills a sponsor wants from an athlete. Your sport already gives people a reason to watch. The craft is turning that attention into content a business would actually pay for.

Step 1: Build a personal brand people can find

Before a business can pay you, it has to find you and get you in about three seconds.

  • Bio: your sport, your school or city, one line on what you post, and a contact email.
  • Handles: the same name on every platform, so a sponsor never wonders which account is really you.
  • One content lane: training, game-day prep, food, faith, study life, whatever is genuinely you. Pick one and post it consistently.

When I presented at Military Creator Con, my "Getting set up" slide covered bio setup, pitch emails and DMs, and picking the best platforms. Athletes need the same basics, just with more ice baths.

High school athletes, one big rule: FHSAA bylaws (opens in a new tab) bar you from using your school's uniforms, logos, mascots or marks in NIL activity. My guide to Florida NIL rules for high school athletes covers the rest.

Step 2: Make sample brand content before anyone pays you

Brands want to see what they'd get. So make it before you ask.

  • Product-in-use demos: a short video of something you already own doing its job. Your post-practice protein shake, your cleats, your recovery routine.
  • Honest reviews only: the FTC's Endorsement Guides say that when an ad says you use a product, you must have been a bona fide user of it (opens in a new tab). Review what you really use. No fake reviews, ever.
  • Hook, show, say: grab attention in the first line, show the product working, then say what you honestly liked.

Label your samples as samples. Showing a product in your own video does not mean that brand hired you, so never say or hint that it did. Same rule I follow on my own page: a product in my video does not mean the brand hired me.

And skip the categories I skip. I don't make content for alcohol, tobacco or gambling. For Florida high school athletes, the FHSAA bans those outright, along with vaping, cannabis, weapons and more.

For format ideas, see my breakdown of types of UGC videos. For how a sponsored post should look once a deal is signed, read my guide to NIL sponsorship content.

Step 3: Pitch local businesses yourself

Local businesses make a natural first stop: the gym, the smoothie shop, the car wash, the family restaurant down the street. Keep the pitch short, specific and polite. Here's a labeled sample structure, not a script that guarantees anything:

Sample DM or email: Hi [owner's name], I'm [your name], a [sport] athlete at [school]. I [use your product or visit your business, only if true]. I'd love to make a short video showing [specific product] for your page. Here's a sample of my content: [link]. Would you be open to talking about a paid post? Thanks for your time!

Before you agree to anything, loop in your compliance office. If you're Division I, the deal may need to go into NIL Go. In Florida high school NIL, the FHSAA requires a written agreement and an affidavit to your school within five business days of signing.

So why do I teach the pitch instead of sending it for you? Because in Florida, a person must hold a valid athlete-agent license (opens in a new tab) to represent a college athlete on NIL contracts. The Florida DBPR (opens in a new tab) licenses athlete agents, and acting as an unlicensed agent is a third-degree felony (opens in a new tab). I'm not a licensed athlete agent. So I coach the content and the pitch, and you, your parents and any licensed professional you choose handle the deal.

Step 4: Disclose and report every deal

  • Disclose in the content. The FTC counts free or discounted products (opens in a new tab) as a material connection, not just cash. Put a clear "#ad" or "sponsored" in the video itself, because the FTC says the disclosure should be in the video (opens in a new tab), not just in the description. Vague tags like "collab" or "ambassador" on their own don't cut it.
  • Report it. Division I athletes: deals of $600 or more go to NIL Go within five business days. You can name one representative to enter deals for you, but you make the submission.
  • Plan for taxes. The IRS says gig income must be reported whether or not you get a Form 1099-K (IRS Publication 525 (opens in a new tab)). NIL pay is income, and gifted product may count too, so talk to a licensed tax professional. I'm not one, and I won't pretend to be.

What is changing in Washington

Federal rules are moving, so here's where things stand as of October 1, 2026:

  • The Senate passed the Protect College Sports Act (opens in a new tab) 77-22 on September 28, 2026. It now goes to the House. It is not law.
  • The SCORE Act stalled. House leadership pulled it from the floor twice, per a Morgan Lewis client alert (opens in a new tab).
  • Key sections of Executive Order 14400 (opens in a new tab) on college sports took effect August 1, 2026.

None of this changes your to-do list today. Follow the rules that apply to you right now, and ask your compliance office when something changes.

Questions people ask

Do I need an agent to get NIL deals?

You can pitch brands yourself, and this guide shows you how. If you want someone to represent you on NIL contracts in Florida, state law requires that person to hold an athlete-agent license, and Florida DBPR handles those licenses. Take any question about representation or your eligibility to your school's compliance office.

Do I have to report my NIL deals?

If you are an NCAA Division I athlete, third-party deals worth $600 or more in total go into NIL Go within five business days, whether or not your school shares revenue. Division II, Division III, NAIA, junior college and high school athletes follow different rules, so ask your compliance office or athletic director.

Are NIL deals taxable?

The IRS says gig income must be reported whether or not you get a Form 1099-K, and NIL pay is income. Ask about gifted product too. I am not a tax professional, so take the details to a licensed one.

What is NIL Go?

NIL Go is the College Sports Commission's portal for Division I deals with outside companies. Deals worth $600 or more in total go in within five business days, and the CSC checks each one for a valid business purpose and a reasonable range of pay. Your compliance office can walk you through it.
