How much do UGC creators make? Ask the internet and you get the Magic 8 Ball answer: "Reply hazy, try again." So let's do this the GenX way, with straight talk, labeled sources and zero hype.

Quick refresher: UGC is short-form video you make for brands about products, or as I like to say, it's how you turn your doom scrolling into money rolling. But nobody pays the electric bill with a catchphrase. You need numbers, and you need to know who published them.

One thing up front: this post does not list my rates or my earnings. Every number below comes from a published rate guide or a platform's own rules, labeled with its source and current as of October 2026. Use them as a starting point for your own math, not as a promise.

## The honest answer: UGC pay varies a lot

There is no official UGC pay scale. Most of the UGC rates you'll find online are published by companies that sell UGC or connect brands with creators. That doesn't make them wrong, but it does mean they have a point of view. So every time I quote one here, I tell you who said it.

What moves UGC creator pricing is the same stuff that moves any freelance quote: your experience, what the brand plans to do with your video, how fast they need it, and whether you're paid per video, per month or per sale.

## What vendor rate guides list in 2026

[JoinBrands](https://joinbrands.com/blog/ugc-creator-rates/), a UGC marketplace, published a 2026 rate guide in August that sorts creators into three tiers, priced per piece of content:

- **Beginner:** $150 to $400 per piece
- **Intermediate:** $400 to $1,000 per piece
- **Established:** $1,000 to $3,000+ per piece

The guide doesn't say how it arrived at those numbers, so treat them as one vendor's view, not a survey. And that little "+" on the top tier means no ceiling is listed.

[[CHART]]

A second vendor blog, [ppc.io](https://ppc.io/blog/ugc-pricing), rounded up what UGC marketplaces charge. As reported there, Collabstr lists $50 to $1,000+ per UGC video depending on the creator, and Billo sells a package of 6 UGC videos for $500. Collabstr's own page wasn't verifiable directly, so that range is ppc.io's report, not mine.

Notice the spread. The low end of one marketplace sits below the beginner tier of another guide. Packages built for brands buying in bulk and creators quoting their own work are not the same product, so the brief matters more than any single number.

### UGC rates for beginners

If you're brand new, the JoinBrands beginner tier of $150 to $400 per piece is one published reference point. Some brands will offer free product instead of pay. Whether you take that is your call, but know this: the FTC treats free product as a connection you have to disclose when you post about the brand. More on that below.

## What pushes a UGC quote up

The base video is only part of the price. The add-ons are where two quotes for the "same" video end up miles apart. Here's what the vendor guides list.

[[TABLE]]

### Usage rights

Usage rights are the big one. Usage means where the brand can run your video and for how long. JoinBrands treats limited usage (one platform for 3 months) as the base rate. It lists expanded usage across all paid channels for 6 to 12 months at an extra 25% to 40% of the base rate, and unlimited perpetual rights at an extra 50% to 100%. ppc.io says some larger creators charge 30% to 50% of the base rate for extended usage rights.

A brand posting your video on its own page for a short window is a different ask than running your face in paid ads forever. Your quote should say which one you're pricing. I break this down in [UGC usage rights: what to get in writing](/blog/ugc-usage-rights/).

### Whitelisting

Whitelisting means a brand runs ads through your account, so the ad carries your handle. ppc.io reports that some brands pay an extra 30% to 100% of the base fee per month for that permission. It's priced monthly because the access is ongoing, and it should end when the agreement ends.

### Raw footage and b-roll

Raw footage is your unedited clips. B-roll is the extra shots, like hands, product close-ups, pouring and unboxing, that an editor uses to build more ads. ppc.io says some creators charge an additional 30% to 50% of the base rate for unedited content. B-roll is a big part of how I like to work (more on that below), so treat it as its own line item instead of tossing it in for free.

### Revisions, rush and exclusivity

These three usually show up as contract line items. I'm not putting numbers on them, because I don't have a source I trust for them and I'm not about to make one up.

- **Revisions:** say how many rounds are included, and what counts as a revision versus a brand new concept.
- **Rush:** if a brand needs the video faster than your normal turnaround, that's a line item.
- **Exclusivity:** ppc.io notes that negotiating exclusive content rights may affect pricing. If a brand wants you to skip its competitors for a while, that's work you're turning down, so price it.

## TikTok Shop pays differently

TikTok Shop affiliate videos don't pay a flat fee. They pay commission on sales, and TikTok spells out the formula in its [seller Academy](https://seller-us.tiktok.com/university/essay?knowledge_id=6077860360177451&lang=en):

> Commission = (Revenue - Refunds) x Commission rate.

Sellers set the commission rate, and TikTok's [collaboration guide](https://seller-us.tiktok.com/university/essay?knowledge_id=6837873164896001) says it can range from 1% to 80%. If a seller invites you directly (a target collaboration), that rate overrides the open rate everyone else sees.

Here's a made-up example, just to show the math. Say a product sells $500 through your video in a month, $50 gets refunded, and the rate is 15%. Your commission is ($500 minus $50) x 0.15, which is $67.50. Same sales at a 5% rate and you're at $22.50. The rate matters as much as the views.

Two rules for newer accounts, per TikTok's [Creator Eligibility Policy](https://seller-us.tiktok.com/university/essay?knowledge_id=6939143037667118&lang=en) as of October 2026:

- Affiliate creators need at least 1,000 followers to self-apply.
- Affiliates with fewer than 5,000 followers go into an Affiliate Creator Pilot Program for at least 30 days. During it, you're limited to 3 shoppable product videos a day and 3 shoppable LIVEs a week, you can only promote products with a Shop Performance Score of 95% or higher, and you can't join campaigns.

These rules change often, so check the live page before you plan around them.

I post TikTok Shop videos myself. A product showing up in my videos is my own content and does not mean that brand hired me. And whenever there's a commission or a free product involved, there's a disclosure involved. The FTC's [Disclosures 101](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers) is plain about it: if a brand gives you free or discounted products and you mention one of its products, disclose it, even if nobody asked you to mention it.

## Retainers vs one-off videos

A one-off video is one brief, one deliverable, one invoice. A retainer is a monthly agreement for a batch of content. Both are legit ways to get paid.

My preferred model is the retainer. In my own words, from my notes: "I want to create a bunch of videos and B roll that I can just upload to a Google Drive and they pay me monthly retainer for that." Batches of videos plus b-roll, delivered to a shared Drive, paid monthly. That's the sweet spot for me.

ppc.io makes a related point from the money side: many creators want long-term deals, which may bring the cost down for the brand but give the creator a steadier income stream than one-off deals.

I'm not listing a retainer price here. A solid retainer quote spells out the number of videos per month, the b-roll, the usage rights and the turnaround, so nobody is guessing on the first of the month.

## How to build a UGC rate card

A UGC rate card is a one-page menu of what you sell and how each piece is priced. Even if you never send it as a PDF, building one forces you to decide your numbers before a brand asks. When I presented at Military Creator Con, my "Getting set up: What You Need" slide covered payment gateways, contracts, pitch emails and DMs. A rate card belongs in that same setup kit.

Here's a simple template. Fill in your own numbers:

1. **Base video:** one edited video at your standard length.
2. **Extra hooks or versions:** the same video with a different opening line, priced per version.
3. **B-roll pack:** a set number of clips for the brand's editor.
4. **Raw footage:** unedited files, as an add-on.
5. **Usage add-ons:** organic only, paid ads for a set window, or perpetual, each as a percentage or a flat add-on.
6. **Whitelisting:** priced per month, with an end date.
7. **Rush and extra revisions:** what each one costs.
8. **Exclusivity:** only if the brand asks, priced by category and length.
9. **Terms:** when you get paid, what's included and how long the brand can use the work.

Then quote every project in writing. A rate card is the starting point, and the brief decides the final number. If usage is the part that trips you up, start with [my usage rights guide](/blog/ugc-usage-rights/).

## Don't forget taxes

Not tax advice, just a friendly heads up. The IRS says in [Publication 525](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p525) that if you did gig work, you must include all the income you received, whether or not you got a Form 1099-K. The IRS page doesn't name UGC specifically, so ask a tax professional how your fees, commissions and any free product apply to you. Keep records from day one. Future you will say thank you.
