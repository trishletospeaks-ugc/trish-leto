Picture this. A brand pays for a batch of videos, the files land in its Google Drive, and the marketing team figures it owns them forever, everywhere, for anything. The creator figured the brand was posting them on its own Instagram for a few months. Nobody lied. Nobody wrote it down either. Cue the awkward email thread.

That gap is exactly what **UGC usage rights** fix. Usage rights spell out where a brand can run a creator's video, whether it can put ad money behind it, and for how long. I'm Trish, a GenX UGC creator, model and Navy veteran in Tampa. I am not a lawyer, so this is the plain-English version from the creator's side of the table: who owns the video by default, the usage tiers brands usually buy, what vendor rate guides say those tiers add, and a checklist for your UGC contract. If creator content is brand new to you, start with my [guide to UGC for brands](/blog/ugc-for-brands/) and come right back.

One quick note before we dive in: this is general information, not legal advice.

## Who owns a UGC video by default?

The creator, unless the paperwork says otherwise. Even a vendor blog written for brands says it plainly: [Hootsuite's UGC guide](https://blog.hootsuite.com/user-generated-content-ugc/) notes that the creator holds the copyright, and that using their content without consent can lead to infringement claims.

So paying for a video and owning the copyright are two different things. What the brand actually gets is whatever the contract grants. There are two main ways that happens.

### UGC licensing vs work for hire

**A license.** The creator keeps the copyright and gives the brand written permission to use the video in specific ways: certain channels, organic or paid, for a set amount of time. Every usage tier in this post is a flavor of license.

**A work made for hire.** Here the brand, not the creator, is considered the author. For a commissioned piece, like a video a brand orders from a freelance creator, the [U.S. Copyright Office's Circular 30](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ30.pdf) explains that it only works for certain types of work (a part of a motion picture or other audiovisual work is one of them) and only with an express written agreement between the creator and the party ordering it. Whether a specific deal qualifies depends on the contract and the facts, so that one is a question for your attorney, not your creator.

## The usage tiers brands buy

Think of usage rights as a menu. Here are the items you will see most often on it.

**Organic only.** The brand posts the video on its own social accounts, and maybe its website, with no ad money behind it. This is the lightest use of the video.

**Paid usage for a set term.** The brand runs the video as an ad for a set window. In its [creator rate guide](https://joinbrands.com/blog/ugc-creator-rates/), JoinBrands, a UGC marketplace, describes limited usage as one platform for 3 months and expanded usage as all paid channels for 6 to 12 months.

**Perpetual rights.** All channels, forever. That is the same guide's top tier, and it is priced like it.

**Creator-account ads.** The industry calls this whitelisting. The official names are [Spark Ads](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/spark-ads) on TikTok and [Partnership Ads](https://www.facebook.com/business/news/meta-at-iab-newfronts-2025) on Facebook and Instagram. The ad runs from the creator's post or with the creator's name on it, which is why it needs the creator's say-so. TikTok's help center says Spark Ads can use organic posts made by other creators with their authorization. Meta said in May 2025 that advertisers can now feature one partner, such as the creator, in a Partnership Ad's header. More on both below.

## UGC usage rights pricing: what vendor guides list

Let me be crystal clear here: these are vendor numbers. They are not my prices, and they are not an industry average. Rate guides publish ranges, and the ranges below come from two of them.

- **JoinBrands** lists expanded paid use (all paid channels, 6 to 12 months) at 25% to 40% on top of the base rate, and unlimited perpetual rights at 50% to 100% on top. The guide doesn't say what data its ranges come from.
- **[ppc.io](https://ppc.io/blog/ugc-pricing)**, another vendor blog, says some larger UGC creators charge 30% to 50% of the base rate for extended usage rights.
- The same ppc.io guide says some brands pay an extra 30% to 100% of the base fee per month to run ads through the creator's account.

In plain math, a 50% add-on means the usage fee equals half the base rate. A 100% add-on doubles the total. The logic is simple: a video that runs on every channel forever does a lot more work for a brand than one that runs on one platform for a quarter, so rate guides price it higher.

## What to put in a UGC contract

Here's the checklist I'd want in front of both sides before anyone hits record. None of it is fancy. It just has to be written down.

1. **Deliverables and formats.** How many videos, how long each one runs, which aspect ratios, how many hooks, and how much b-roll.
2. **Channels.** Organic social, website, email, paid ads, TikTok Shop. List every one you plan to use.
3. **Paid or organic.** If ad spend is going behind the video, say so in plain words.
4. **Term and territory.** A start date, an end date, and the countries where the video can run.
5. **Creator-account authorization.** Which posts, which platform, and for how long. TikTok lets advertisers customize the duration of a Spark Ads authorization code, so put a real number of days in the deal.
6. **Edits.** Can the brand trim it, recaption it, add text or music, or cut new versions from it?
7. **Raw footage and b-roll.** Are the unedited clips included, and do the same usage terms cover them?
8. **Exclusivity.** If the creator can't work with competitors, for how long, and in which product categories?
9. **Payment terms.** The amount, the due dates, and whether it is a monthly retainer or a one-time project.
10. **Disclosure.** Who adds the #ad, and where. The [FTC's Disclosures 101](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers) tells creators that if a brand gives them free or discounted products or other perks and they mention its products, they should make a disclosure. And under the [FTC Endorsement Guides](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/255.1), advertisers can be liable for misleading endorsement claims or for failing to disclose material connections. Both sides have skin in this game.

## Spark Ads and Partnership Ads permissions

Creator-account ads deserve their own section, because the permission works differently from a file sitting in your Drive.

On TikTok, Spark Ads let an advertiser run organic TikTok posts as ads. When the post belongs to another creator, that creator has to authorize it, and TikTok lets the advertiser customize how long the authorization code lasts. Here's a detail worth knowing: TikTok says all of the Spark Ads post engagement is attributed to the original organic post. The likes, comments and shares stack up on the creator's post, not on a separate ad.

On Facebook and Instagram, the equivalent is Partnership Ads. At the 2025 IAB NewFronts, Meta announced that advertisers can now feature one partner in the ad's header, where both the advertiser and the partner used to be listed.

What to write down: which posts, which ad accounts, the start and end dates, and what happens to any ads still running when the term ends. The full setup lives in my [TikTok Spark Ads guide](/blog/tiktok-spark-ads/), and if you're planning the creative itself, my post on [UGC ads](/blog/ugc-ads/) covers hooks and scripts.

## Questions to ask before you sign

Run through these before anybody signs anything:

- Is this a license or a work made for hire, and does the contract say so in writing?
- Which channels are covered, and is any of it paid?
- When does usage end, and what happens to live ads on that date?
- Is creator-account access included, and for how long?
- Do we get raw footage, and are we allowed to edit?
- Is there exclusivity, and is it priced in?
- Who handles the disclosure, and how?

This is general information, not legal advice. Have an attorney review contracts.
