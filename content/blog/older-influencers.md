## Older influencers: who counts, and why it matters

Let's clear something up first. "Older influencer" is not an official title. Nobody hands you a membership card at the door. It is simply what people type into a search bar when they want creators who look and live more like them: grown-ups with real kitchens, real schedules and real opinions. You see it in searches like "influencers over 50" and "fashion influencers over 50," and in phrases like GenX influencer or UGC creator over 40.

I'm Trish, a GenX UGC creator, model, public speaker and Navy veteran in Tampa. I would rather talk about GenX audiences than anybody's birthday, so here is the working definition I use. Pew Research Center defines [Generation X as people born between 1965 and 1980](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2019/01/17/where-millennials-end-and-generation-z-begins/). Add up the Census Bureau's 2024 population estimates for the ages that line up with those birth years and you get about 65.6 million Americans. That is our own calculation from the [Census single-year-of-age file](https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/popest/datasets/2020-2024/national/asrh/nc-est2024-agesex-res.csv), not a published Census "Gen X" number, and the age window only approximates the birth years. Still, that is a whole lot of people who remember rewinding a cassette with a pencil.

So when I say "older influencers" in this post, I mean creators who speak to that grown-up crowd. The label is not the interesting part. The data behind the lane is.

## The money is grown-up

Here is the part brands tend to underrate. In the Bureau of Labor Statistics Consumer Expenditure Surveys for 2024, households headed by someone in Gen X were 26.7% of US households but accounted for 32.9% of all consumer spending, per the [BLS generation tables](https://www.bls.gov/cex/tables/calendar-year/aggregate-group-share/reference-person-age-generation-2024.xlsx). That is a bigger slice of the spending than of the headcount.

Zoom out to everyone 50 and up and the numbers get louder. [AARP's Longevity Economy Outlook 2026](https://www.aarp.org/money/personal-finance/longevity-economy-report-2026/) reports that households headed by someone 50 or older account for more than half of all US consumer spending: $10.7 trillion in 2024. The same report puts the economic activity of Americans 50 and older at $12.5 trillion in 2024, which is 43% of US GDP.

A few honest footnotes, because I'm not here to oversell you. The BLS figures describe households by the birth year of the person who heads them, not individual people. The AARP numbers cover everyone 50 and older, so they include plenty of folks who are not GenX. And none of this proves any one creator will sell anything. What it does show is that the grown-up wallet is not a niche. It is most of the store.

## Grown-ups are on the creator apps

"Sure, Trish, but are grown-ups actually on TikTok?" Fair question. Pew Research Center's 2025 survey of US adults says yes, more than a lot of people assume. Per its [Social Media Fact Sheet](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/social-media/), 62% of adults ages 30 to 49 use Instagram and 44% use TikTok. Among adults 50 to 64, 40% use Instagram and 30% use TikTok.

[[CHART]]

Yes, younger adults use these apps more. Nobody is arguing that. But 30% of the 50 to 64 crowd on TikTok is not a rounding error, and the same Pew table shows 85% of that age group on YouTube and 74% on Facebook. Grown-ups scroll. We just do it with our reading glasses pushed up on our heads.

For a creator, that means your people are already in the room. For a brand, it means a grown-up face in a video is not a stretch. It is meeting buyers where they already hang out.

## Older influencer or UGC creator?

This is where a lot of people over 40 get stuck, so let me untangle it. "Influencer" and "UGC creator" get tossed around like they are the same job. They are not. In common industry usage, as [Hootsuite's vendor blog](https://blog.hootsuite.com/user-generated-content-ugc/) puts it, UGC creators make content for the brand's channels, while influencers post content on their own.

### The influencer lane

An influencer sells access to an audience they built. The brand pays for a post on the creator's account, seen by the creator's followers. Follower count and engagement matter a lot here, because the audience is the product.

### The UGC creator lane

A UGC creator sells the content itself. The brand gets the video files and runs them on its own pages and in its own ads, to its own customers. That means you do not need a big following to do UGC, which is great news if you are starting from zero at any stage of life.

One exception worth knowing: some platform programs do set follower minimums. TikTok Shop's US affiliate program, which pays commission on sales from shoppable content, says creators must [have a minimum of 1,000 followers](https://seller-us.tiktok.com/university/essay?knowledge_id=6939143037667118&lang=en), per TikTok Shop Academy as of October 2026. Those rules change often, so check the current policy before you plan around it.

You can absolutely do both jobs. Plenty of creators post on their own feed and also make UGC for brands. I break the differences down further in [UGC vs influencer: which one a brand needs](/blog/ugc-vs-influencer/).

## How GenX creators stand out

Here is what I think gives grown-up creators an edge. This is my take as a creator, not a study, so read it as advice from a friend who does this work.

### Lead with humor and honesty

GenX grew up on sarcasm and healthy skepticism. We can smell a fake from a mile away, and so can the people watching your videos. Be funny, be direct and say what you actually think. My whole pitch for this business is to "turn your doom scrolling into money rolling," and that only works when the person on screen sounds like a real human.

### Shoot where real life happens

You do not need a studio. The kitchen counter, the garden bed, the driver's seat of your car: those are the sets grown-up buyers recognize, because that is where they use the stuff. A brand selling a blender wants to see it next to the coffee maker, not floating in a white void.

### Pick the niches grown-ups buy

Play to what you know. The categories I work in are supplements and food, pet products, car products, kitchen gadgets and appliances, planting and gardening, and makeup, skin care and hair care. Grown-ups buy all of those, and grown-ups tend to believe other grown-ups about them. The categories I won't touch: alcohol, tobacco and gambling. Know your own lines before a brand asks.

### Only show what you really use

This one is not just good manners. It is the rule. The FTC's guide for influencers says plainly that [you can't talk about your experience with a product you haven't tried](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers). So if you rave about a serum, you had better have used the serum. And no miracle claims, ever. When a brand gives you anything, free product included, disclose it in the video. A product showing up in one of my videos means I use it. It does not mean that brand hired me.
