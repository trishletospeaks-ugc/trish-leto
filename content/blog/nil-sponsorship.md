Let's get one thing straight before we talk money: an NIL sponsorship is mostly a content job. A business pays you for the right to use your name, image and likeness, and what it usually gets back is a post. That happens to be my lane. I'm a GenX UGC creator and Navy veteran who makes short-form videos for brands, so this guide takes what I know about product content and points it at athletes and the families behind them.

Quick ground rules. This is education, not legal or tax advice. I don't find, pitch, negotiate or sign deals for anyone, and nothing here promises a deal, a dollar amount or a clearance.

## What an NIL sponsorship really buys

Opendorse, a commercial NIL platform, published a report in July 2025 called NIL at Four. It found that of the commercial NIL dollars athletes receive, 80% come from services such as posts ([Opendorse, NIL at Four](https://biz.opendorse.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/NIL-at-Four-Monetizing-the-New-Reality_July2025.pdf)). In other words, most NIL brand deals pay for content.

The newer numbers point the same way. Per Opendorse's 2026 report, as summarized by Future of College Sports, the average deal is $6,200. It's a one-off 84% of the time, it's paid in cash 96% of the time, and the deliverable is usually a single piece of Instagram content ([Future of College Sports](https://futureofcollegesports.substack.com/p/new-opendorse-releases-2026-annual)).

Keep the source in mind, though. Opendorse says its insights cover 200,000+ athlete users and $250M+ in NIL pay from 100,000+ NIL activities ([Opendorse](https://biz.opendorse.com/annual-nil-report-2026/)). That's a big pile of data, but it's one platform describing its own users, not the whole market. Your deal may look nothing like the average, and that's fine.

So before you say yes to any sponsor, get these answers in writing:

- **The deliverable.** How many posts or videos, on which platform, in what format.
- **The timing.** When it goes live, and how long it has to stay up.
- **The message.** What the brand wants you to say, and what you won't say.
- **The disclosure.** How the post will tell viewers it's paid.
- **The usage.** Whether the brand can reuse your video in its own ads, and for how long. Brands should read my take on [UGC usage rights](/blog/ugc-usage-rights/) for why that matters.

## Why brands want athlete content

Short answer: people actually pay attention to athletes. In the same July 2025 report, Opendorse puts student-athletes' social media engagement rate at 5.6%, compared with 1.9% for traditional influencers, or about 3.7 times higher ([Opendorse, NIL at Four](https://biz.opendorse.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/NIL-at-Four-Monetizing-the-New-Reality_July2025.pdf)).

Take that with a grain of salt, since it's one platform measuring its own users. But it explains the appeal of NIL social media. Your followers know you, cheer for you and show up for you, and that attention is what a sponsor is really paying for.

Here's my take as a creator: engagement is earned one post at a time. A stiff, scripted post that reads like a billboard wastes the one thing the sponsor came for. The good news? Making content that people actually watch is a learnable skill, and that's what the rest of this guide is about.

## Content that passes the business-purpose bar

If you're an NCAA Division I athlete, your sponsored post has a second audience: the College Sports Commission. Every D-I athlete must report third-party NIL deals worth $600 or more in total, whether or not their school opted in to revenue sharing ([College Sports Commission](https://www.collegesportscommission.org/about)). Those deals go through a portal called NIL Go.

NIL Go checks whether a deal has a valid business purpose. In plain English, the CSC wants the deal to use your NIL to promote or endorse goods or services sold to the general public for profit, and it wants direct activation of your NIL rights ([CSC, NIL Go](https://www.collegesportscommission.org/nil/)). The CSC also warns about something it calls *warehousing*: when a collective or other associated entity pays for your rights without explaining how it will use them, that deal may not satisfy the rules.

My reading, as a content person and not a lawyer: a real post is the most visible proof that a deal is real. The sponsor sells something to the public, and you actually promoted it. A few habits make that easy to show:

1. Get the deliverables in writing before you film.
2. Save the live link and a screenshot of every sponsored post.
3. Keep the brief, your script and your disclosure with the contract.

None of that guarantees a deal clears. That's the CSC's call, and your school's compliance office is the right place for questions about your own situation.

## Making the post: borrow from UGC

Here's the fun part. Sponsored athlete content is basically UGC with a jersey number, so the formats brands already run work for you too. I break them down in my guide to [types of UGC videos](/blog/types-of-ugc-videos/), but these are the ones that fit NIL content creation best:

- **Talking head.** You, the camera and a straight answer to "why do you actually use this?"
- **Day in the life.** The product shows up where it really fits your routine, not jammed into the frame.
- **Hands-on demo.** Show how the thing works, up close.
- **B-roll.** Short clips the brand can cut into its own ads, if your deal allows it.

Want hooks and a simple script structure? My [UGC ads guide](/blog/ugc-ads/) walks through a video beat by beat, from the hook to the call to action.

### Only promote what you actually use

Under the FTC's Endorsement Guides, if an ad says you use a product, you must have been a bona fide user of it when you gave the endorsement ([16 CFR 255.1](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-255/section-255.1)). So no faking it. If you've never tried it, you can't tell your followers it's part of your routine. Keep your claims to your honest experience.

### High school? Watch the logos and the banned list

Florida high school athletes can do NIL under FHSAA Bylaw 9.9, but the content rules are tighter. School uniforms, logos, mascots and marks are off limits in NIL activity. The bylaw also bans whole product categories, including alcohol, tobacco, vaping and nicotine products, cannabis, gambling (sports betting included), weapons and NIL collectives ([FHSAA Handbook](https://s3.amazonaws.com/fhsaa.org/documents/2024/11/20/2425_handbook_update_11424.pdf)).

That text comes from the 2024-25 handbook, so confirm the current version with your athletic director before you film anything with school gear in it. Parents, my post on [Florida high school NIL rules](/blog/florida-high-school-nil-rules/) covers the rest.

## Disclose it right

This is the part people skip, and it's the part that can bite. Under the FTC's Endorsement Guides, a material connection to a brand has to be disclosed, and that includes free or discounted product, whether or not the brand asked you to post ([16 CFR 255.5](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-255/section-255.5)). The disclosure has to be clear and conspicuous, which the FTC defines as difficult to miss and easily understandable by ordinary consumers ([16 CFR 255.0](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-255/section-255.0)).

[[TABLE]]

The short version:

- **Use plain words.** "Ad" or "sponsored" does the job. The FTC's guide for influencers says to skip vague terms like "sp," "spon" or "collab," and stand-alone words like "thanks" or "ambassador" ([FTC, Disclosures 101](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers)).
- **On video, put it in the video.** The same guide says the disclosure should be in the video, not just in the description. Say it, and put it on screen.
- **Free stuff counts.** A box of gifted product is a material connection, even with no cash involved.
- **Use the platform tool too.** TikTok defines branded content as content that promotes a brand in exchange for payment or any other incentive, and it asks creators to turn on its commercial content disclosure setting. Its policy also says you are responsible for making sure your branded content follows the law ([TikTok Branded Content Policy](https://www.tiktok.com/legal/page/global/bc-policy/en)). The tool is a backup, not a replacement for your own words.
- **No fake reviews.** The FTC's rule on reviews and testimonials makes it a violation for a business to write, create or sell reviews or testimonials that materially misrepresent the experience behind them ([16 CFR 465.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-465/section-465.2)). Don't be the face of one.

And the same honesty runs the other way. When a product shows up in one of my own videos, that's my content, not proof that the brand hired me. Your followers deserve the same clarity from you.

## Free product is still income

Gifted gear feels free. The IRS may not see it that way. IRS Publication 525 says you must include in your income the fair market value of property or services you receive in bartering ([IRS Publication 525](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p525)). A product-for-post deal can look a lot like that.

I'm not a tax pro, so here's my entire tax advice: keep a simple list of what you received, when, and what it was worth, and ask a tax professional how it applies to you.

## For local businesses sponsoring athletes

Own a gym, a cafe or a car wash and thinking about NIL marketing with a local athlete? Love that. Here's what I'd want in writing as the brand:

- **A clear deliverable.** The number of posts or videos, the platform and the dates.
- **A real product or service you sell to the public.** For college athletes, that's what the valid business purpose check looks at.
- **A disclosure plan.** Plain words, inside the video.
- **Usage rights,** if you want to run the video as an ad later.
- **Realistic expectations.** A post buys you content and attention. It doesn't promise a sales number.

Then send your compliance questions to the athlete's school. College athletes have NIL Go and a compliance office, and high school athletes have their own FHSAA rules. You run the business; let the school run compliance.
